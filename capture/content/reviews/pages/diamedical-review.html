01 · What I was asked to do

Review the experience.
Go deeper on the products.

I was asked to identify what works well and suggest improvements across DiaMedical’s website. The assignment then asks for a closer review of Hospital Beds & Stretchers: how products are presented, how filters work, and how useful the content is. Search, metadata, and SEO provide additional areas to consider.

I organized the response around one purchase a real buyer would make:

“I need a reconditioned bed rated for 500 lb. Which ones, and what will they cost?”

It is a question the catalog can answer. Fourteen products in Hospital Beds & Stretchers state 500 lb or more and are listed as reconditioned. The filters as they stand today reach eleven of them, because three carry no capacity or condition value the facets can read; and none of the fourteen shows a price, so the answer to the second half is a phone call. Following that one question is what produced the strengths and the opportunities in this review.

02 · The problem I investigated

Useful information.
Moments of uncertainty.

DiaMedical already provides product discovery, educational guidance, detailed specifications, and procurement support. I examined how those pieces agree when a customer moves between them.

Read the five strengths

The clearest example is the quote journey. In the captured comparison, Vantage and Stryker S3 With Scale displayed $0.00 and Add To Cart. Their product pages displayed Call for Price and Add To Quote.

The labels disagree. That could make the next step unclear, but its effect on customers still needs a usability check. Other findings include repeated specifications that conflict and a product missing from the tested capacity filter despite matching its stated capacity.

My question is how to make the information more consistent while preserving the useful tools and content already there.

04 · How I did it

Observe. Record.
Explain. Test.

The review starts with public evidence. The interactive examples use a local sample built from those observations.

Inspect all 20 samples
  1. 01

    Inspect the experience.

    I moved from the wider website into Hospital Beds & Stretchers, then followed selected products through product pages, filtering, and comparison.

  2. 02

    Keep an evidence record.

    I retained public URLs, screenshots, page text, product details, and sampled metadata. Each sample has an observation date and links back to its saved evidence.

  3. 03

    Explain the finding.

    I separated what I observed from why it might matter, what I would change, and how I would check it. I included strengths as well as opportunities.

  4. 04

    Build a focused example.

    I used the six product records to demonstrate selected ideas. Proposed aliases and display rules stay separate from the captured source information.

  5. 05

    Repeat the same task.

    I held the sample and rules constant, made one local change, and compared the result. Conflicting specifications remain open for confirmation.

05 · How the numbers are made

Three questions.
Three separate answers.

The numbers help explain the demonstration. Each answers a different question.

Inspect every scoring reason

How complete is this record?

0–100

Record completeness combines six weighted field checks: identity 25%, taxonomy 25%, content 20%, SEO 15%, merchandising 10%, and provenance 5%.

Example: applying three suggested Vantage aliases raises local completeness from 75 to 80. It adds merchandising credit; it does not verify the specifications.

Why did this product match?

Ranking points

Search adds points for matching fields such as SKU, title, alias, category, and use case. The page lists the reason for each contribution and shows the product’s position in the sample.

For “simulation bed”, those aliases add 240 points: 290 becomes 530, moving Vantage from second to first in this six-record example.

Did the local change pass its checks?

Pass / open

The quote example checks the comparison label, agreement across all four demonstrated views, and whether a product with a published price keeps that price unchanged.

Applying the quote rule changes the result from 1 of 3 to 3 of 3. This is a local validation result; approval and production testing are still separate steps.

How the website itself was built

I built the review as a React and TypeScript website using Vite. Saved evidence and structured sample records support the findings, product comparison, and scoring examples. Browser state holds local selections and drafts; CSV and JSON exports make that work inspectable.

The quote, capacity, and alias controls run against the local sample. The website has no connection to DiaMedical’s internal catalog or analytics. Public observations, proposed changes, and calculated local results remain separate.
